Doubling Luxembourg's highways

What would residents of the Greater Region pay for twice the capacity on every highway link inside Luxembourg? This dashboard shows the model's answer, together with the traffic, speeds and vehicle-kilometres behind it, under four ways the economy can adjust.

Companion to Raian Kudashev and Pierre M. Picard, Welfare Effects of Congestion in Luxembourg and the Greater Region (working paper). Every number is read from the saved model outputs; nothing is re-estimated in the browser.

Beta version. This dashboard will be updated as the paper develops and more results come in. Feedback is very welcome: r.kudashev@vu.nl.

Who gains, by country

Willingness to pay is minus the compensating variation: the income a resident would give up each year to keep the doubled highways. Tax revenue follows workplaces, so Luxembourg collects most of the fiscal gain even though residents across the border gain as well.

Six places up close

Traffic and vehicle-kilometres

Capacity relief shortens trips at first, but once workplaces and homes respond, people commute further. The time saving almost disappears while vehicle-kilometres keep rising, and traffic moves off primary roads onto the highways.

Checking the model against Duranton and Turner (2011)

Elasticities with respect to highway capacity, set against the US estimates in Duranton and Turner's "The Fundamental Law of Road Congestion". Model entries are log changes divided by log 2, because capacity doubles.

Standard errors of the published estimates are in parentheses; model entries are deterministic. The treatments differ: Duranton and Turner measure physical lane-kilometres, while the model raises effective capacity on existing links, keeping network length fixed. The model counts commuting trips only.

About this dashboard

This page accompanies the working paper by Raian Kudashev and Pierre M. Picard. The quantitative spatial model combines recursive-logit route choice on a simplified road network with location and workplace choice across 1 km cells in Luxembourg, France, Belgium and Germany. Congestion follows a BPR volume-delay function, t = t̄(1 + α(f/k)β), with α and β estimated from traffic-camera data. The counterfactual doubles capacity k on the treated highway links inside Luxembourg.

  • Only rerouting: commuter origins and destinations and all economic variables stay at baseline; only routes, flows and travel times respond.
  • Static economy: residents stay where they live; workplaces, jobs, wages, floorspace prices and routes adjust.
  • Closed city: residents may also move, with total population fixed.
  • Open city: total population adjusts too, keeping utility at the outside option.

Credits: route-choice formulation after Fosgerau, Frejinger and Karlström (2013); validation against Duranton and Turner (2011), American Economic Review 101(6); the BPR volume-delay function of the US Bureau of Public Roads. Data sources are listed in the paper. Traffic figures are commuter trips in vehicle equivalents: one modelled commute leg per road commuter.
